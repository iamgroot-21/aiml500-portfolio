Evolution of AI and Machine Learning: 1950 to Today

Key milestones, the two AI winters, and the rise of deep learning, cloud computing, and generative AI

⬇ Download PPTX
🎯

Objective

This timeline traces the major milestones in artificial intelligence and machine learning from Alan Turing's foundational 1950 paper through the 2024 Nobel Prizes awarded for AI-related research. By mapping these events chronologically, the artifact helps the audience understand how cycles of enthusiasm and disillusionment (the two AI winters) shaped the field, and how the convergence of algorithms, data, and compute after 2012 triggered the current era of generative AI.

🏷

Eras of AI

Birth of AI 1950–1973 ❄ Winter 1 Expert Systems 1980–1986 ❄ Winter 2 ML, Data & Cloud 1993–2011 Deep Learning & Cloud 2012–2021 Generative AI 2022–
⏳

Timeline

Turing Test

❄️ The AI Winters

First winter (about 1974–1980). Early systems such as perceptrons hit hard limits, computers were too weak, and promises outran results. The UK and U.S. governments cut research funding and many labs shrank.

Second winter (about 1987–1993). Expert systems were expensive to maintain and broke outside narrow cases, and the specialized Lisp machine market collapsed. Companies failed, funding fell again, and researchers avoided the "AI" label.

🚀 Why Deep Learning Took Off After 2012

Three things came together: algorithms (backpropagation, CNNs, transformers), data (the web and ImageNet), and compute (NVIDIA GPUs and cloud platforms such as AWS). Our World in Data shows training computation doubling about every six months since 2010. Google's PaLM (2022) used more than 5 million times the training compute of AlexNet (2012).

Machine Learning vs. Deep Learning: Choosing the Right Approach

Two real-world cases, spam filtering and retinal image screening, and why each calls for a different kind of model

View report (PDF) ⬇ Download Word
🧭

Skill Demonstrated

Technical judgment: matching a modeling approach to the data and the problem. Artifact 1 shows how I research and tell a story. This artifact shows how I decide which tool fits the job.

🎯

Objective

This report compares machine learning and deep learning through two real-world cases to show when each approach is the better choice. It answers a question analytics teams face in practice: before building a model, which kind of model fits the data and the problem?

✉️ Case 1: Spam detection (machine learning)

Real-world use. SpamAssassin, an open-source spam filter, uses a Bayesian classifier that learns from the words in emails marked as spam or not spam.

Why it fits. The useful signals are easy to name, such as suspicious phrases, unusual sender domains, and odd formatting. The data is modest in size, models like naive Bayes train quickly and cheaply, and it is easy to see why a message was flagged.

Why deep learning does not. It would need far more data and computing power, it would be hard to explain a flagged email, and it can overfit when the important features are already known.

👁 Case 2: Retinal screening (deep learning)

Real-world use. Screening retinal photographs for diabetic retinopathy, an eye disease linked to diabetes. An FDA-authorized system called IDx-DR uses this kind of technology to flag patients who need an eye exam.

Why it fits. The input is raw pixels, and the signs of disease, such as tiny bleeds and subtle changes in blood vessels, are hard to write as rules. A convolutional neural network learns its own features in layers, from edges and textures up to shapes like lesions.

Why traditional ML does not. Someone would have to design features by hand for every type of lesion, which is slow and would likely miss subtle patterns.

This report shows that I choose a modeling approach to fit the problem, and I do not default to the most complex one. For a healthcare analytics team, that means weighing accuracy against explainability, data needs, and cost, and being clear about where human review is still needed. It builds on my BI work, where the best model is often the simplest one that answers the business question.
